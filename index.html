About Me

My career has strategically built an interdisciplinary profile at the forefront of digital chemistry, integrating materials science, electrocatalysis, chemical automation, and AI for sustainable energy discovery. I specialize in the development of automated platforms for the controlled stimulus of electro- and photochemistry, utilizing integrated analytical feedback to drive autonomous discovery.

During my PhD, I pioneered automated in-situ analysis to understand complex electrochemical processes at nanointerfaces, leading to a real-time gas monitoring device and a granted patent. I also designed an in-flow electrochemical cell for the ALBA synchrotron enabling real-time electrocatalysis and XANES measurements, complemented by Raman, X-ray spectroscopy and confocal microscopy studies.

My postdoctoral work at the University of Glasgow expanded into digital chemistry with the ElectroChemputer, the first universal framework for programmable electrosynthesis. Since September 2024, I have served as Postdoctoral Researcher and Team Leader of the Chemputer Reactivity Team in the Cronin Group. In this leadership role, I manage multidisciplinary projects focused on advancing reactivity and chemical discovery through robotics.

Outside the lab, I enjoy bringing science to life through illustration, video editing, and 3D modelling. Some of my artwork has even made it onto the front covers of scientific journals.

I’m very happy to share that our article, “Support-Controlled Reaction Pathways via Dynamic Sulfur-Mediated Interfaces for Selective Urea Electrolysis,” has been published in Advanced Functional Materials 🥳 This work is especially meaningful to me, as it started during the first years of my PhD in Santiago de Compostela. Looking back, it represents many experiments, discussions, challenges, and learning moments that helped shape me as a researcher. In this work, we show that catalyst supports are not passive spectators. By using sulfur-doped graphitized carbon nanofibers, we demonstrate that dynamic sulfur-mediated interfaces can steer urea electrolysis pathways and improve selectivity toward N₂. This provides a novel control of the reaction pathway through adaptive catalyst–support interactions. I’m grateful to all the co-authors who made this work possible. My first co-author Lucía Vizcaíno Anaya, Ahmad, Jose and my PhD supervisor Maria Giménez. Thank you for support, and contributions throughout the process. Chemical automation is only as good as the tools holding it together, and fragmented device handling remains one of the biggest bottlenecks in automating chemistry labs. PyLabware tackles this and more with a unified, capability-based framework that standardises communication across preparative and analytical devices. With support for 40+ commercial and in-house instruments from 20+ manufacturers - such as rotary evaporators, hotplates, stirrers, and NMR spectrometers - it's built to scale with your chemistry, not against it, providing simple reaction control and detailed reaction insights. I am very happy to share that our paper, "ElectroChemputer with integrated monitoring for programmable electrochemistry," is now published in Chem Cell Press Given the fact that electrosynthesis introduces a multitude of additional electrochemical reaction parameters (many of which are unfamiliar to traditionally trained chemists), this clearly demonstrates the urgent need for unified hardware and software approaches that streamline the development and optimization of electrosynthesis without restricting the accessible (electro-)chemical reaction space. To tackle this, we developed a modular platform that encodes electrochemical procedures into executable digital scripts. Here, we extend the digital chemistry infrastructure into the electrochemical domain by introducing hardware-agnostic control, standardized protocol execution, and real-time reaction monitoring within a unified software environment. The peer review process was incredibly constructive and rewarding. The reviewers highlighted the platform's flexibility and describing the breadth of the work as a "tour de force in electrosynthesis". One reviewer specifically pointed out our approach to generating hydrogen and oxygen gases in situ as a promising way to avoid storing hazardous reagents. By applying a plateau-detection algorithm to the course of the Jaccard similarity index, we establish an automated endpoint detection. To the best of our knowledge, this method has not been used together with electrochemical synthesis before. Most importantly, we have made our tools more accessible to the community. We detailed a minimal setup and provided open-source 3D-printable files for our reactor lids. This ensures anyone can replicate our standardized setup for reproducible preparative synthesis, even without building the fully automated robot. A big thank you to my co-authors for their hard work Robert Rauschen Kristine Laws Abhishek Sharma Niclas Grocholski Mark McNulty and Lee Cronin Read the open-access paper here: https://lnkd.in/eCjeTe9e A huge thanks to Carsten Streb's preview of our paper in the same issue: https://lnkd.in/eG67PbSN #Electrochemistry #Automation #DigitalChemistry #ChemCellPress #OpenScience #Robotics

🎉 I’m delighted and profoundly relieved to share that one of the key projects from my PhD research has been published in Advanced Materials! Working on this publication took intense dedication, and seeing the results finally published in such a high-impact journal is incredibly rewarding. The paper, titled "POM-Based Water Splitting Catalyst Under Acid Conditions Driven by Its Assembly on Carbon Nanotubes", focuses on the challenge of developing stable, earth-abundant, and efficient bifunctional electrocatalysts for water electrolysis under harsh acidic conditions. Our findings detail a novel vanadium POM material whose catalytic activity can be precisely switched from oxygen to hydrogen evolution by simply controlling its assembly on carbon nanotubes. I want to express my gratitude to all co-authours, collaborators and supervisor for the fantastic team work: María Giménez, Eugenia Pilar Quirós Díez, PhD, Carlos Herreros Lucas Alejandro López Moreno José Manuel Vila-Fungueiriño, Antonio L. Llamas-Saiz, Karol Strutynski, and Manuel Melle-Franco. Research

My research connects digital chemistry, robotic experimentation, electrochemical methods, and materials discovery. I am interested in systems where hardware, chemical reactivity, analytical feedback, and algorithmic decision-making can be treated as one programmable experimental workflow.

  • Programmable electrochemistry and electro-photo-chemical platforms
  • Robotic workflows for reproducible chemical synthesis and analysis
  • Polyoxometalate materials, electrocatalysis, and energy-related chemistry
  • Data-rich experimental design for automated discovery
